Flooded basement clean up in Birmingham

Safe pump-out, silt and debris removal, drying, disinfection and mould treatment for flooded basements, cellars and lower ground floors. Covering Birmingham and the wider West Midlands, whatever the time.

Please don't go down there yet. There's a good chance the electrics are wet, and the water may be deeper than it looks.

A flooded basement or cellar behaves differently from a flooded ground floor. Water has nowhere to go, so it sits. The air is still and cool, so drying is slow. The room often houses the things you least want wet: the fuse board, the gas meter, the boiler, the washing machine, years of stored belongings. Water pressing on the walls from the outside can also make pumping out riskier than it looks.

If you're reading this with water at the bottom of the stairs, your first questions are probably these. Is it safe to go down? Where is the water coming from? Can I pump it out myself? Will my insurer pay? How do I stop it happening again? This page takes them in turn, and explains what a proper basement clean-up involves once the water is gone.

Call us and a team can be arranged, with the price confirmed in writing before the work starts.

What's included

  • A safety check of access, electrics and gas supply before anyone goes down
  • Controlled pumping of standing water, with the pace set by how the building is coping
  • Removal of silt, mud, debris and ruined stored items
  • Stripping of materials that cannot dry, such as carpet, underlay, plasterboard, insulation and stud walling
  • Cleaning and, where the water was dirty, disinfection of floors and walls
  • Drying with dehumidifiers and air movement suited to a closed space, with moisture readings taken as it progresses
  • Mould and odour treatment
  • Advice on why it flooded and what could stop it happening again
  • A dated record of the work, with photos and moisture readings, to hand to whoever is paying

Why did it flood?

The cause matters more in a basement than anywhere else, because it affects how risky the water is, who pays, and whether it will happen again.

  • Heavy rain and surface water running down steps, light wells or through doors and air bricks
  • Rising groundwater, which seeps through walls and floors and can be slow and persistent
  • Mains pipework, a header tank or the heating system giving way
  • A failed sump pump, or a power cut that stopped it working
  • A backed-up drain or sewer, where foul water comes up through floor drains, gullies or a basement toilet: see sewage clean-up
  • Water coming down from a flat or house next door or above
  • Failed waterproofing, such as damaged tanking or a blocked drainage channel
  • Flooding elsewhere in the house that has drained downhill: see flood damage clean up

Is it safe to go down?

Often not, and the dangers are not obvious.

  • Electrics. Water and sockets, fuse boards, boilers, freezers and extension leads are a lethal combination. If the consumer unit or any wiring could be in or near the water, don't go in. Ask your electricity supplier or an electrician to isolate the supply first.
  • Gas. Meters and boilers in cellars can be damaged by water. If you smell gas, leave and call the National Gas Emergency number on 0800 111 999.
  • Deep water. You can't see steps, holes, floor drains or debris under the surface. Water more than knee-deep is dangerous.
  • Dirty water. If it came from a drain, toilet or river, it can carry sewage and bacteria. Avoid contact, and keep children and pets away.
  • Stale or contaminated air. A closed cellar can have poor air. Fumes from stored chemicals, fuel or a running petrol pump are a risk, and a petrol or diesel pump should never be run indoors.
  • Heavy items. Water-soaked boxes, furniture and plasterboard are heavier than they look.

If you aren't sure, stay upstairs, keep other people out, and call us. We can talk through the risks.

Why not just pump it all out yourself?

For a few centimetres from a burst pipe, a small submersible pump or a wet vacuum may well be enough. For deeper water, particularly from groundwater or heavy rain, pumping needs care.

  • Water outside can push in. When the ground around a cellar is saturated, the water inside helps balance the pressure on the walls and floor. Emptying the cellar too quickly can stress the structure. A professional team pumps in stages and checks the building as it goes.
  • Water can come straight back. If the water table is high, pumping out the cellar can simply refill it. It is worth knowing whether the source has stopped.
  • Silt and sludge don't go through a standard pump. They have to be shovelled out while still wet, before they set.
  • Hired pumps need power, and extension leads in wet areas are dangerous.
  • Where the water goes matters. Don't pump onto a neighbour's land or into a surface-water drain if it's dirty. Dirty water should go to the foul sewer.

If you have a sump pump, check whether it has failed and why, before relying on it again.

What to do while you wait

  1. Stay out until the electrics are safe.
  2. Cut off the supply if a pipe is the culprit,using the stopcock, or isolate the appliance.
  3. Take a picture of the water linefrom the top of the stairs, time-stamped if you can.
  4. Ring your insurer or landlord.Ask whether they have a preferred contractor, and what they need to see.
  5. Move anything from upstairsaway from the stairwell, as damp air rises.
  6. Leave doors and air bricks as they areuntil we've advised you. If it is dirty water, don't spread it around the house.
  7. Call usand tell us what you can see from the stairs.

What happens after the water has gone

The pump-out is the quick part. What's left behind is more of a problem.

  • Silt and mud. Flood silt is often contaminated. It sets hard as it dries, so it's best cleared early.
  • Soaked walls and floors. Brick and stone hold water for a long time. Plasterboard, insulation and chipboard soak it up and have to come out.
  • Stored belongings. Cardboard, fabric, books and papers rarely survive. Photograph them, then sort what can be dried or cleaned and what can't.
  • Drying a closed space. A cellar has little airflow, so drying is slow and relies on dehumidifiers, air movement and moisture checks. Opening a window can bring in more damp air than it removes.
  • Mould and smell. Both can take hold within days, especially in a dark, cool space. Cleaning and drying thoroughly is what stops them coming back.
  • Damp that was already there. Some cellars were damp before the flood. We'll tell you if the readings suggest an ongoing problem, as that needs a damp or waterproofing specialist.

Stopping it happening again

We clean and dry. We don't install waterproofing or drainage, but we can tell you what we noticed, so you can ask the right specialist:

  • Sump pump and battery backup, so a power cut doesn't leave it stranded
  • A non-return valve on drains that can back up
  • Tanking or a drained cavity system if groundwater is the problem
  • Clearing gullies, gutters and drains that send water towards the building
  • Sealing air bricks and light wells that take surface water
  • Raising stored items off the floor

Structural waterproofing is a specialist job, and quotes vary widely, so get more than one.

Claiming on insurance

What follows is a rough guide, and your policy wording is what counts.

  • Cause decides cover. Escape of water from a pipe, storm damage and groundwater flooding can be treated differently under a policy. Check your wording.
  • Ring first. Tell your insurer before clearing anything away, and photograph first.
  • Basements can be limited. Some policies treat basement or cellar contents differently, or exclude certain causes. Ask directly.
  • Keep evidence. Dated photos, receipts, moisture readings and the quote all help a claim.
  • List what was stored down there, with rough values.
  • No insurance? Say so when you ring. The team can help you prioritise, and your council may know of local support.

Tenants, landlords, basement flats and businesses

Tenants. Put it in writing to your landlord or agent straight away, and shift your belongings somewhere dry if it is safe to do so.

Landlords and agents. If the basement is lived in, such as a lower-ground flat or converted room, you will want to act quickly and keep a record of everything. See the landlords page.

Basement flats and conversions. Habitable rooms below ground are the most exposed. If you live there, tell us whether you can stay elsewhere while it dries.

Businesses. Cellars used as stores, cold rooms or plant rooms can hold stock and equipment worth saving. Give us a list and your trading hours.

Pests. Floods drive rodents into buildings. If you notice droppings or signs of rats, see after rodent clean up.

Flooded Basement Clean Up FAQs

Can a flooded basement be saved?

Usually, yes. Brick and masonry dry out. Plasterboard, insulation, carpet and chipboard often have to come out. The sooner the water is dealt with, the better the outcome.

What if the water is sewage?

Stay out and call us. Dirty water means disinfection and the removal of anything porous it has touched. See our [sewage clean-up page](/sewage-clean-up/).

How long will it take to dry?

Surfaces can feel dry in days, but cellar walls and floors often take weeks. We'll monitor moisture and tell you when it's safe to repair.

Can I use a wet vacuum and a dehumidifier?

For a small, clean spill, yes. For anything deeper, dirtier or persistent, the equipment and the pace matter, and mistakes can make it worse.

Is it dangerous to pump a basement dry?

It can be, if groundwater is high. Rapid pumping may stress the walls and floor. A professional pumps in stages and watches the building.

Can you stop it happening again?

We clean and dry, and tell you what we notice. Waterproofing, drainage and sump pumps need a specialist.

Will my insurance cover it?

Sometimes. It depends on the cause and the policy, and some policies limit basement cover. Call your insurer before you clear anything.

Do you work in flats and commercial cellars?

Yes. Tell us who owns and manages the space and who can approve the work.

How soon can someone come?

Our line is answered around the clock. If it's an emergency, say so, and you'll be told the soonest realistic time.
