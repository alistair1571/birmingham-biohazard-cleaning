Who pays for a hoarder clean up?

Published 8 October 2026

The short answer

Usually whoever is responsible for the property and its contents: the person who lives there, the landlord of a rented home, or the executor of an estate. There is no national fund that pays for hoarding clearance, councils rarely carry the bill for a private home, and home insurance almost never covers it.

That can sound bleak if you're a daughter looking at your mother's house or a landlord who has just been through a tenant's front door. But who pays isn't a single answer. It depends on whose home it is, who has the right to make decisions about it, and which agencies are already involved. Most people also have more options for spreading or reducing the cost than they expect.

This guide goes through the common situations in turn. It is general information, not legal or financial advice, and it covers England. Rules differ in other parts of the UK.

If it's your own home

If you own or rent the home you live in and you want help, you are the one who decides and who pays. Options that can reduce the strain:

  • Do it in stages. A single room, then the next, rather than the whole house at once. It spreads the cost, and for many people it's easier emotionally.
  • Start with safety. Clearing the exits, the kitchen and the bathroom first gives the biggest benefit for the least money.
  • Ask family or friends to help with the cost, or with the sorting, if you feel able to. Time given by people you trust can reduce the hours a team needs.
  • Ask your GP about support. Hoarding disorder is a recognised mental health condition, and talking therapies exist. Clearing the space without support often doesn't last.
  • Talk to Hoarding UK (hoardinguk.org). It offers free, confidential advice and can signpost local help.
  • Check what your council offers. Some councils help with practical support or small grants in particular circumstances. It isn't guaranteed, and varies by area.

If you're a family member

This is where most people get stuck. If your parent, sibling or partner lives in a hoarded home and won't or can't pay, you generally have no automatic right to clear it, and no automatic duty to pay.

  • You can't clear it without their agreement. Binning someone's possessions over their objections is upsetting for them and can also land you in legal trouble.
  • You can offer to pay. Many families do, either in full or by sharing the cost between siblings.
  • A Lasting Power of Attorney changes things, if one is in place and registered for property and financial affairs. The attorney can make spending decisions on the person's behalf, but must act in their best interests. A solicitor can explain the position.
  • If they lack the capacity to decide, a social worker or the GP may need to be involved. Hoarding can be a form of self-neglect under the Care Act 2014, and adult social care has duties where someone is at risk.
  • Raise fire-safety worries with the fire service. The local brigade runs free home visits for people at risk, and hoarded homes are exactly the kind it looks at.

If the person won't engage at all, talk to their GP, adult social care or Hoarding UK, who can help you think about the options. See our hoarders clean up page for more on approaching it gently.

If the person has died

When someone dies and leaves a hoarded home, it falls to the executor to deal with the contents, and the bill normally comes out of the estate. In practice:

  • The cost is usually met from the estate before it's shared, rather than by the family personally.
  • Keep an itemised quote and receipts. The executor or the solicitor will want them.
  • If the property is rented, the landlord and the executor will have to agree who does what. Tenancy documents and the landlord's insurer may be relevant.
  • Valuables, papers and photographs can be hidden in clutter. Tell the team what you are looking for, and agree in advance how items found will be handled.

Our page on after death cleaning goes through the sequence.

If you rent from a private landlord

A tenant is generally responsible for keeping the home in a reasonable condition and for damage they cause. If hoarding blocks exits, causes pests or damages the property, the tenancy agreement may have been breached.

  • Landlords can't just clear a tenant's belongings. They need the tenant's agreement, or to follow the proper legal process. Removing them without it can create serious problems.
  • The tenant can be asked to pay, or the cost can be claimed from the deposit if the tenant caused damage. Evidence matters: the inventory, dated photos, a written notice to the tenant and quotes.
  • The deposit may not cover it. Hoarding clearance can cost more than the deposit, and recovering the rest may mean a court claim, with no guarantee of payment.
  • Landlord insurance rarely covers clearance of a tenant's belongings. It may help with specific damage, such as fire or flooding, depending on the policy.
  • Environmental health may get involved. Councils can take action where a property is a risk to health, which can include pests and hoarding.

Tenancy law is changing, so a landlord association or solicitor should confirm the current position before anyone acts. We cover the landlord's side in more depth on our landlords and letting agents page.

If you rent from a council or housing association

This varies a lot between landlords.

  • Many social landlords have a hoarding policy and a tenancy support team. They usually start with support, not enforcement.
  • Where a property is a fire or health risk and the tenant won't engage, a social landlord can take enforcement action under the tenancy. The tenant may be charged for the work, either upfront or added to their rent account.
  • Some will arrange and fund a clearance as part of a support plan. Others will only help if the tenant takes up the support offered.
  • Items in shared areas are usually cleared promptly, and the tenant may be charged.

If you're a tenant and worried, speak to your housing officer early. Asking for help is nearly always better received than waiting to be found out.

What about the council?

Councils don't have a general duty to pay for hoarding clearance in a private home. What they can do depends on the department and the risk.

  • Environmental health can act where a home is a risk to health or has pests, and may be able to serve notices on an owner. Councils can sometimes carry out work themselves and charge the owner, but recovering the costs is often difficult.
  • Adult social care assesses people who may need support, including those who neglect themselves. It may arrange support, but doesn't automatically fund clearance.
  • Housing and the fire service deal with safety, fire risk and tenancy matters.

Birmingham's own research into hoarding found that environmental health was involved in about half of the cases it looked at, that it was often unclear which agency should lead, and that clearing clutter on its own treats symptoms but not causes. That research dates from 2016, so approaches may have changed, but the pattern is familiar: several departments, each with part of the picture, and no one paying by default.

If you think a neighbour's home is a risk, you can contact the council and the fire service. If it's your own relative, going through their GP or adult social care is usually more productive than waiting for enforcement.

Does insurance pay for hoarding clearance?

Rarely. Home and landlord insurance policies cover specific events, such as fire, flooding, theft or malicious damage. Hoarding is generally a gradual condition, not an insured event, and many policies exclude neglect.

There are some exceptions worth checking:

  • If a fire or flood has happened in a hoarded home, the cost of cleaning the damage may be covered, although the amount of contents and the state of the property may affect the claim.
  • Landlord insurance may help with specific damage, depending on the wording.
  • Some legal expenses policies help with tenant disputes.

Read the wording or ask the insurer. Don't assume cover, and don't assume a claim is impossible.

What affects the cost?

There's no standard price per room, because hoarded homes are so different. Four things move the figure most:

  • How much there is, from one crowded room to a whole house
  • What else is in the mix. Droppings, mould, rotting food or human waste mean slower, more protected work
  • How awkward it is to reach, such as upper floors, tight stairs or nowhere to park
  • Crew size and time on site, and how much waste has to leave, including anything that needs special disposal

Whether it's tackled in one push or in phases also changes how and when you pay.

You can usually reduce the bill by sorting valuables and papers yourself beforehand, being clear about what stays, and clearing in stages. We don't give a figure without seeing the property, because any figure given blind is a guess.

How to get a fair quote

  1. Ask for it in writing and itemised,so you can see what's included and compare.
  2. Send photos,or ask for a visit. A good quote needs to see the problem.
  3. Ask what is and isn't covered:waste disposal, deep cleaning, odour treatment, valuables handling.
  4. Ask how contaminated waste is disposed ofand what paperwork you'll get.
  5. Be wary of firms that quote a flat price without seeing the property,or that ask for large sums in cash upfront.
  6. Get more than one quoteif you have time, and check what each includes.

Who else can help with the cost?

  • Family and friends, by sharing the bill or the sorting
  • Charities and support groups, such as Hoarding UK, which can point to local help
  • Your council or housing association, especially if you're a tenant
  • Adult social care, if the person has care needs
  • Mental health services, which sometimes support clearance as part of treatment
  • Local community or charity grants, which vary and are rarely large

None of these is a guarantee. Ask early, and be prepared to be told that there is no funding.
