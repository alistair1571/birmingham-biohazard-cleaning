Does home insurance cover biohazard cleaning?

Published 8 October 2026

The short answer

Sometimes. There is no standard "biohazard cleaning" cover. Whether a policy pays depends on what caused the problem, because most policies insure specific events (fire, escape of water, malicious damage) and then pay for the clean-up that follows. They tend not to pay for problems that built up over time, such as neglect or hoarding.

So the useful question isn't "does my insurance cover biohazard cleaning?" but "what happened, and is that an insured event under my policy?" This guide goes through the common situations, what insurers usually want to see, and what to do if a claim is turned down. It's general information, not insurance or legal advice. Your policy wording and your insurer's decision are what count.

Start with the type of policy

  • Buildings insurance covers the structure and fixtures: walls, floors, ceilings, built-in kitchens and bathrooms. It's the policy that usually responds to damage such as an escape of water, a fire or a storm.
  • Contents insurance covers your belongings: furniture, carpets you own, clothing and personal items. Contaminated contents may be covered if the cause is an insured event.
  • Landlord insurance is a separate product, often with its own buildings and contents sections and optional extras, such as cover for malicious damage by tenants or loss of rent. Wording varies a lot.
  • Tenants' contents insurance covers the tenant's own things, not the landlord's property.
  • Commercial or business policies have their own terms, and can include business interruption cover.

If the property is rented, ask who holds which policy before you ring anyone.

What insurers usually pay for, and what they usually don't

This is a rough guide to how policies tend to treat common situations. Always check your own wording.

SituationHow it is often treated
Escape of water from a burst pipe or applianceUsually covered as an insured event, including drying and cleaning, subject to the excess and conditions
Sewage backing up into the homeOften covered where it comes from your own drains or pipes. Check for exclusions and the cause
Flood from rivers or surface waterOften covered under buildings and contents, but check the wording and any flood limits
Fire and smokeUsually covered, including cleaning and restoration
Malicious damage or vandalismMay be covered, but damage by tenants or people you let in is often excluded unless you add specific cover
Death at home or a violent incidentNot an insured event in itself. Cleaning may be covered if it follows an insured event, such as a crime that caused damage. Policies differ
Hoarding, neglect or long-term contaminationRarely covered. Gradual damage and neglect are commonly excluded
Rodents and other pestsOften excluded from standard cover. Some policies and emergency add-ons help with specific pests
Cannabis cultivation by a tenantOften excluded on landlord policies unless you have specific optional cover
Needles and sharps left behindSeldom covered as a standalone problem

One published landlord policy document we looked at, for example, covered escape of water and malicious damage, excluded damage by tenants unless an optional extension was added, excluded drug production by a tenant unless extra cover was bought, did not cover gradual damage, and limited cover if the property was left unoccupied for more than 60 days. It's one example, not a rule, but it shows how much hangs on the small print.

Why the cause matters so much

Insurers distinguish between a sudden, accidental event and a gradual problem.

A pipe that bursts overnight is an event. A drain with a known defect that leaks for months is not, and a claim for the damage may be refused as wear and tear. The Financial Ombudsman Service upheld an insurer's decision to decline an escape-of-water claim in just that situation, where earlier reports had identified drain defects that hadn't been repaired. The lesson: fix known faults promptly and keep the records.

Similarly, a house filled with rubbish over years is not an event, whereas a fire or flood in a hoarded home is.

Common situations in more detail

After a death at home

Insurers don't treat natural death as an insured event. Home insurance will rarely pay to clean up after one. Where the cleaning is needed because of something else that is covered, such as an escape of water or a fire, the claim may be handled under that cover. If the home is now empty, tell the insurer, because policies often have conditions for unoccupied properties. Our guide to after death cleaning covers who normally pays.

After a crime or trauma

If a crime caused covered damage, such as a break-in with vandalism, the insurer may pay for repairs and cleaning of the damaged parts. The blood and bodily fluids clean-up may or may not be included. Get the crime reference number and ask the insurer directly. Our crime scene cleaning page explains the police-release stage.

Sewage and drains

Sewage that comes up through your own drains is often treated as an escape of water or a similar insured event, but exclusions exist. If the problem came from the public sewer, the sewer owner's responsibilities and your insurer's cover may both apply. Our sewage clean-up page explains who does what.

Rented properties

Landlord insurance may help where a tenant has caused damage, but only if the policy includes the right cover. Many exclude illegal activity such as cannabis growing, and loss of rent has limits and conditions. Tenants should tell the landlord and check their own contents policy. See our landlords and letting agents page.

How to improve your chances of a successful claim

  1. Ring the insurer first,before you discard anything or book work. Many insurers want to inspect the damage or appoint their own contractor.
  2. Ask what they will cover and in what order.Get it in writing if you can.
  3. Photograph and film everythingbefore it is moved or cleaned, with dates.
  4. Keep the evidence:crime reference numbers, fire service reports, drainage reports, correspondence and receipts.
  5. Ask for an itemised quotefrom any cleaner, so each item can be matched to the claim. We provide one, along with before-and-after photos and waste paperwork.
  6. Keep receipts for emergency costs,such as hotels and clothing. Insurers sometimes reimburse them.
  7. Do what the policy requires,such as protecting the property from further damage. Failing to may reduce the claim.
  8. Make an inventoryof damaged contents with approximate values.

Should you claim at all?

Not every problem is worth claiming for.

  • The excess. You pay the first part of the claim. A small clean-up may not exceed it.
  • Premiums. A claim can raise your renewal price or affect a no-claims discount.
  • The record. Claims can show up when you apply for cover elsewhere.
  • The alternative. For smaller jobs, paying directly can be simpler.

Weigh the likely cost against the excess and the effect on your premium. If you're unsure, ask the insurer what would be covered before you decide whether to make a claim.

Using your insurer's contractor or your own

Some insurers insist on their own approved contractors. Others let you choose, but may want to approve the quote first. Ask which applies before you commit. If you use your own contractor, make sure they can supply an itemised quote, dated photos, a written report and the waste carrier paperwork, because that is what the insurer will want to see.

If the claim is declined

  1. Ask for the decision and the reasons in writing,and the policy wording they relied on.
  2. Check the reasons.Was it excluded, or did they say it was gradual, or not an insured event?
  3. Complain to the insurer.They usually have up to eight weeks to give a final response.
  4. Go to the Financial Ombudsman Serviceif you're unhappy with the final response or they haven't replied in time. You must do so within six months of the final response letter. The service is free, and you don't need a lawyer.
  5. Keep a recordof every call and letter.

A free helpline or consumer advice service can help you word a complaint, and a solicitor can advise where a large sum is involved.

What if there's no cover?

Many people end up paying for biohazard cleaning themselves, especially after a natural death, hoarding or neglect. Options that can help:

  • Ask for itemised quotes from more than one provider, and compare what each includes.
  • Do it in stages, if the property allows.
  • Check who is responsible. An executor, landlord or estate may be the right party to pay.
  • Check other policies, such as legal expenses, or a tenant's contents policy.
  • Talk to the council or a charity if you're facing hardship. Support varies and isn't guaranteed.
