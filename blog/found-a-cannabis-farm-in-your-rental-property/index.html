Found a cannabis farm in your rental property? What to do next

Published 8 October 2026

If you've just learned that your rental property has been used to grow cannabis, you're probably dealing with several problems at once: whether it's safe to go in, whether you're in trouble, what happens to the tenancy, whether insurance will pay, and how much damage there will be. The honest answer to most of them is "it depends", but the order in which you act matters a great deal.

This guide is for landlords and letting agents in England. It sets out what to do in sequence, from the moment you find out through to getting the property clean, repaired and back on the market. It is general information, not legal advice, and some of the rules, especially around tenancies, are changing. For decisions about possession, entry, belongings or your own liability, talk to a solicitor or a landlord association.

Suspicion versus discovery

There are two different situations, and they call for different responses.

You suspect a grow. Perhaps a neighbour has complained about a sweet smell, the windows are blacked out, there's a constant hum, or an inspection was refused. Do not confront the tenant or try to get in to check. Growers can be dangerous, and the building may have bypassed electrics or fire risks. Report your concerns to the police on 101, or anonymously to Crimestoppers on 0800 555 111. Call 999 if there is an immediate danger, violence or a threat to life.

You've found one, or the police have called to say they've raided it. Skip ahead to the checklist below.

The warning signs, in case you missed them

None of these proves anything on its own, but together they should prompt action:

  • Windows blacked out, covered with foil or permanently curtained
  • A strong, sweet smell, quite unlike cannabis smoke
  • Constant fan noise, or ducting running out of a window or through the roof
  • Condensation, mould or peeling paint where there was none before
  • Altered wiring, a tampered meter, or an electricity bill that has jumped, or dropped to nothing
  • Lights on day and night in a loft or back bedroom
  • Soil, fertiliser, plant pots or bin bags of roots outside
  • Visitors at odd hours, a tenant who avoids inspections or insists on paying cash

The checklist: what to do, in order

1. Don't go in until the police say so

Cannabis farms regularly have dangerous wiring, high humidity, mould and sometimes chemicals. The property may also be a crime scene. If the police have raided it, they will tell you when it is released. If they haven't been involved, don't enter on your own. If you must go in, take a witness, and don't touch the electrics.

2. Get a crime reference number and the officer's details

You'll need them for your insurer, your lender, your agent and anyone you deal with later. If you can't get through to the officer in charge, keep asking, and keep a written note of every call.

3. Don't disturb anything the police have sealed or left

Wait for confirmation that you can enter, and ask what has been seized and what has been left. Plants, and sometimes equipment, are taken. Often the lights, ducting, soil and waste are left behind.

4. Understand your own legal position

Under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971, someone concerned in managing premises who knowingly allows the production of drugs there can commit an offence. That is aimed at people who knew and let it happen. If you didn't know and acted promptly when you did, you're in a very different position, but your records matter. If you have any worry that you might be implicated, take legal advice early.

5. Tell the people who need to know

  • Your insurer, straight away. Check what your policy says about cannabis cultivation, malicious damage, unoccupied properties and loss of rent. Some policies exclude it, some cap it, and some ask for evidence of tenant checks and inspections.
  • Your mortgage lender, if there is one. Check your terms.
  • Your letting agent or managing agent, if you use one.
  • The council, if the property is an HMO or in a licensing area, as they may ask questions about the property's management.
  • The electricity supplier or network operator. Meters are often bypassed, and unpaid or stolen electricity can lead to bills and investigations. Report the tampering, and don't reconnect anything yourself.

6. Get the electrics made safe

Before the power is switched back on, a qualified electrician needs to inspect the installation. Bypassed meters, new cabling and overloaded circuits are a fire and shock risk. Don't let anyone work in the property until they've said it is safe.

7. Photograph and record everything

Before anything is moved, take dated photos and video of every room, including the loft, the meter and the fuse board. Keep copies of the tenancy agreement, the inventory, the inspection reports, the tenant's references and any messages. If a dispute arises over the deposit, the insurer, the lender or the tenancy, this is your evidence.

8. Sort out the tenancy before you change anything

This is the part where landlords most often make mistakes.

  • An arrest isn't the same as the tenancy ending. The tenant may still have the right to occupy, and may return, for example after being bailed. Don't assume abandonment without clear evidence.
  • Don't change the locks or throw out belongings without the proper process. Doing so risks a claim for unlawful eviction. Possessions the police haven't seized remain the tenant's property.
  • If the tenant has plainly left, or says they've surrendered the tenancy, get that confirmed in writing, and take advice on the next step.
  • If they're still in occupation, possession has to go through the proper legal route. Section 21 no-fault notices were abolished from 1 May 2026, so grounds-based possession through the courts is the route. A solicitor or a specialist such as a landlord association can advise on the right ground and the process.

9. Arrange the clean-up in the right order

Once the property is released and the electrics are safe, the work usually runs in this order: clearance of equipment, soil, chemicals and plant waste; stripping out ruined materials; treating mould and drying; then an electrician's rewiring, structural repairs and redecoration. Our cannabis farm clean up page explains what each stage involves.

Getting the cleaning done first matters because it lets the electrician and builder see the real damage. Painting over a damp wall is a classic waste of money.

10. Chase the costs

  • Insurance. Check the policy, keep all quotes and invoices, and ask in writing what they'll cover.
  • The tenant. The deposit may not go far, and chasing a tenant who has disappeared may not be worthwhile. Take advice on what's realistic.
  • Utilities. Query any large unexplained bills with the supplier, and keep your evidence that you didn't know.
  • Lost rent. Check whether your policy includes it. Many don't.

What does this typically do to the property?

The damage in a former grow house is mostly caused by moisture, heat and improvised wiring:

  • Mould in walls, ceilings and the loft
  • Soaked plasterboard and insulation
  • Holes cut through ceilings, floors or walls for ducting and cables
  • Scorched or tampered electrics
  • Chemical containers, soil and plant waste
  • A smell that persists

It can take weeks or months to bring a property back to a lettable standard, which is why the rent you lose can end up being the biggest cost.

How to reduce the chance of it happening again

  • Reference tenants thoroughly, including employment, credit and previous landlords.
  • Use an experienced local agent, if you're not managing it yourself.
  • Inspect regularly, giving proper written notice. Resistance to inspections is a warning sign in itself.
  • Include the right clauses in the tenancy agreement, and keep a good inventory with photographs.
  • Watch the meter and the bills. A sudden jump or drop in electricity use is a red flag.
  • Listen to neighbours. They often notice smells, noise and comings and goings before you do.
  • Check your insurance wording, and whether it requires inspections.

When you're ready for the clean-up

A professional clean-up won't repair the wiring or the structure, but it will clear the property, treat the mould and give you the records an insurer or lender may want. Get in touch and describe what you've found. A specialist team can assess it from your photos or a visit, and the cost is itemised in writing before any work is booked.
