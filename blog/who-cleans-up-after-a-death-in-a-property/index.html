Who cleans up after a death in a property?

Published 8 October 2026

The short answer

Nobody does it automatically. The police, the coroner, the ambulance crew and the funeral director all have jobs to do after a death, and none of those jobs includes cleaning the property afterwards. Once they've finished, the responsibility passes to whoever is responsible for the home: the owner or their estate, the landlord if it was rented, or the housing provider if it was a social tenancy.

That sounds simple until you're the person standing outside the door. A daughter who has just lost her father, a landlord with a tenant's keys in his hand, and a neighbour who can smell something through the wall each have a different question, and the answer is different for each. This guide goes through who is responsible in the situations people actually find themselves in, who isn't, and how the bill is usually settled. It's general information for England, not legal advice, and rules differ in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

First, do you need a specialist clean at all?

Not every death at home leaves anything to clean.

  • A death with no contamination. If someone died peacefully in bed and was found soon afterwards, there may be no fluids, no odour and nothing beyond normal tidying. The family or executor can usually deal with it as they would any house clearance.
  • A death that wasn't discovered for some time. Fluids can soak through bedding into mattresses, carpets, underlay and floorboards, and the smell goes with them. That needs a specialist clean. Our after death cleaning page explains what the work involves.
  • A death involving injury, violence or a traumatic scene. Blood and other body fluids are a biohazard, and the property may be a police scene first. See crime scene cleaning.

If you aren't sure which one you're facing, a few photos from the doorway, or simply a description, is usually enough for someone experienced to tell you.

Who is not responsible

It helps to rule people out, because families often wait for someone who isn't coming.

  • The police. They attend sudden deaths, make the property safe and may secure it. They don't clean it. When they release it, it's handed back as it is.
  • The coroner. The coroner's role is establishing the facts and the cause of death. A coroner may refer a sudden death for investigation, and the body can't usually be moved without their agreement. They don't arrange cleaning.
  • The funeral director. They take the person into their care and arrange the funeral. They don't clean the home. Many can recommend cleaners.
  • The council. If nobody can arrange a funeral, a council can arrange a public health funeral, and it will usually secure the property while it looks for a will, relatives and valuables. One council policy we read states plainly that it "is not responsible for clearing or cleaning the property", and it seeks to recover its funeral costs from the estate. Policies vary between councils, so check your own, but don't assume it will do the cleaning.
  • Neighbours and other residents. If the home is a flat, the smell may reach them, but they aren't responsible for dealing with the cause.

If the person owned their home

Responsibility sits with the estate, and in practice with the person administering it.

  • If there's a will, the executor named in it deals with the property and its contents.
  • If there's no will, a close relative usually applies to become the administrator, and the rules about who can do that follow a set order. A solicitor or the probate service can explain where you stand.
  • Until someone has legal authority, the family can still arrange a clean for health and safety reasons, and many do. Say clearly who is instructing, and keep the paperwork, because whoever pays will want to recover the cost from the estate.

Two things matter for executors in particular. The property still has to be looked after during probate, and executors can be held personally responsible for mistakes, including failing to meet the conditions of an insurance policy. Neither is a reason to panic, but both are reasons to get the clean done, documented and paid properly, rather than leaving a contaminated or empty property sitting there.

If they rented from a private landlord

This is the one that causes the most confusion. A tenant has died, there may be no obvious next of kin, and the landlord is holding the keys.

  • The landlord is usually the one who arranges the clean, because the property is theirs, it can't be relet as it is, and they have the insurance question to deal with. They can then look to the estate and any deposit for the cost, but recovering money is a separate matter and not guaranteed.
  • The tenancy doesn't end by itself in every case. It depends on the kind of tenancy and whether anyone, such as a partner or family member, has a right to stay. A joint tenant, for example, may continue. Ask a solicitor or a landlord association before assuming anything.
  • The tenant's belongings are not the landlord's to throw out. They belong to the estate. The executor or next of kin normally removes them. Guidance from one council for private landlords (written for Scotland, though the principle is a sensible one anywhere) says to stay in close contact with the family, confirm in writing the date the property must be emptied, and not pressure them. If there's no one to claim them, ask for legal advice before disposing of anything.
  • Rent is a separate conversation. Housing Benefit usually stops from the date of death, so a landlord whose tenant relied on it may be left with arrears. How that is dealt with is for the landlord, the estate and their advisers.
  • Insurance. A death is not, in itself, an insured event, but some landlord policies respond to related damage. Read our guide to whether insurance covers biohazard cleaning.

If you're a landlord or agent, our landlords and letting agents page sets out who has to agree to the work, what paperwork to keep, and the first things to do.

If they rented from a council or housing association

A social landlord has set procedures, and they are worth following exactly.

  • They need to be told as soon as possible, usually with a copy of the death certificate and details of who is handling the estate.
  • A joint tenant may be able to stay. If the tenant lived alone, the tenancy normally has to be ended, typically with four weeks' notice, though practice varies.
  • Rent usually continues until the keys are returned. Two landlords we looked at confirmed this, and noted that Housing Benefit stops from the date of death, so the estate may owe a few weeks' rent.
  • The home must be returned empty. That means furniture, carpets, curtains, rubbish, and anything in the loft, shed or garden.
  • The estate is charged for what is left. One council states that it charges the estate for clearing, cleaning or other work that the next of kin should have done, and another housing association says it may charge for disposing of anything left behind. If the home needs a specialist clean first, you will need to arrange it, or ask the landlord whether it does.

If you're the next of kin and money is the worry, ask the landlord early. At least one council provides a "no funds" declaration where the estate can't pay the rent owed.

If it was a flat

Flats add a complication: other people. Odour can travel through floors, walls and shared corridors, and neighbours have the right to ask what's happening.

  • A leasehold or private flat is still the responsibility of the owner or estate, as described above. If the smell is spreading, tell the managing agent or freeholder, because they may need to know about shared areas.
  • A rented flat follows the landlord route above.
  • Be prepared for questions. A short, factual statement from the managing agent or landlord is better than silence. It need not include any detail about the person.

A specialist team will treat the source, but it's worth telling them if the odour has spread, because it changes the plan.

If there's no one at all

Sometimes there's no will, no family and nobody to act. In that situation:

  • The council may arrange a public health funeral and secure the property.
  • The property and the estate may go through the government's route for estates without known relatives (known as bona vacantia). One council's published policy describes referring the case on when money is left over after its costs are repaid.
  • A solicitor or the property's landlord may end up being the practical contact.

For a landlord or housing provider caught in this position, take legal advice about who has authority to authorise work before spending money you may not recover.

Can the family clean it themselves?

You can, but we don't recommend it where there are body fluids. They carry a risk of infection, they soak further than they appear to, and household cleaning products don't deal with them. The smell is also very difficult to remove without removing the contaminated materials, and contaminated waste shouldn't go into household bins.

If there are no fluids and the job is a general tidy and clear-out, it's a different matter, and family can often handle that themselves. If it's emotionally too much, nobody should feel they have to.

Who pays, and when

Most of the time the bill ends up with the estate, the landlord, or, where it's a social tenancy, the estate charged by the landlord. In practice:

  • Estate. The executor or administrator pays and recovers it from the estate. Ask the solicitor how to do this if the funds aren't yet accessible. Banks can sometimes release money for funeral costs, and a solicitor can say what applies to cleaning.
  • Family member. Many people pay the first invoice personally and claim it back from the estate later. Keep itemised invoices and receipts.
  • Landlord. Pays up front and recovers what they can from the deposit, the estate or their insurer.
  • Insurance. Sometimes helps. See the guide above.
  • Nobody. If there's no money anywhere, tell the person you're dealing with. Some councils and housing providers have processes for estates that can't pay, and a solicitor can advise.

An itemised written quote is the single most useful document, because it can go to an executor, a landlord, an insurer or a solicitor without being rewritten.

What to do in the first few days

  1. Find out who has legal authorityand write it down: the executor, the administrator, the landlord or the housing officer.
  2. Ask the police or funeral director whether the property has been released.Cleaning can't begin until it has.
  3. Secure the propertyand tell the insurer if it's empty. Policies often have conditions about empty homes.
  4. Photograph the propertybefore anything is moved, with dates. It helps with insurers, landlords and estates.
  5. Keep the keys safe,and decide who is allowed in.
  6. Ask for an itemised quoteand don't feel rushed into agreeing on the day.
  7. Look after yourself.Admin on top of grief is exhausting. If you're struggling, Samaritans are free to call on 116 123, day or night.
